## From Describing to Specifying: Reflections on Designing Dynamic Posters for a Virtual AI DJ Idol Game

"Anyone can ask for 'a generative poster that feels unsettling.' Getting something you actually want out of that request is a different skill." I’d like to reflect on this issue by drawing on a real-world project of mine. I was responsible for designing a dynamic poster for an online multiplayer game featuring an AI DJ idol named “Jamai.” The game’s concept centered on a virtual idol born from data streams who reconstructs herself through musical beats; players could interact with her and freely explore the dance floor. While the poster was ultimately a success, the process was far from smooth.

### Version 1.0: "A dynamic poster of an AI DJ idol—cool and high-tech."
That was the initial prompt I gave Midjourney—just that single sentence.

The resulting image was a textbook example of a "cyberpunk template": a neon blue-and-purple color scheme, a young woman wearing glowing headphones, electronic textures in the background, and streaks of flowing light. While it was certainly "cool" and "high-tech," it could have been any character from any game. It wasn't "Jamai."

Where does the problem lie? "AI DJ idol" is a character label, not the character itself. "Cool" is an atmospheric descriptor, not a visual rule. "Tech-inspired" is a stylistic keyword, not a generative logic. I am describing my feelings about the result, not the structure that produces those feelings.

This, I believe, is what -`description` amounts to: it points toward a feeling; the language is poetic and open-ended; and the outcome is one-off and static. At this stage, I am merely a client—fundamentally no different from any passerby who says, "I want a cool poster."

### Version 2.0: A more "specific and detailed description":

"A virtual DJ girl figure whose body is composed of dynamic music spectrum data bars, with hair made of flowing fiber-optic strands, set against a background of a spinning disc in a dark space."

The resulting image was visually closer to what I needed, yet it still felt static and stiff. To the AI, the word -`dynamic` merely implies a visual style that *looks* dynamic; in reality, the image contains no actual movement, change, or responsiveness.

Perhaps I should specify a system that operates continuously—an entity that unfolds over time and reacts to inputs (such as audio).

### Version 3.0: From "Describing Images" to "Specifying Concrete Systems"

I opened my notebook and began writing down these specific instructions—much like drafting a script—using a blend of natural language and code.It avoids buzzwords like "cool," "high-tech," or "star-power," instead organizing intent through variables, parameters, events, and conditions:

The final generation process for the "Jamai dynamic poster":

1. Write `index.html`, `sketch.js`, `particle.js`, and `audio.js` in VS Code.

2. Open the project in a browser using the Live Server extension.

3. The browser handles rendering, while the microphone or an audio file provides real-time data.

4. Output: A dynamic poster running in the browser that reacts to sound.

### Returning to the initial question: is this skill programming, design, or something else?

I view it as the intersection of programming and design, though more precisely, it is a capability involving the integrated application of both. -`Design"`tells me what is "right"—enabling me to envision the final look of a poster; -`programming` allows me to grasp concepts like variables, parameters, functions, events, and conditions, enabling me to translate design intent into executable system rules.

So, why am I here? What this course will teach me isn't how to have AI write code for me, but rather how to become someone capable of precisely articulating my intent using a "specified language."
